## Summary

The record of two years Thoreau spent living alone in a self-built cabin on Walden Pond, tracking exactly what a life actually costs when you account for it honestly — food, shelter, and above all the "quiet desperation" of a life spent earning things you didn't need in the first place. Less a wilderness memoir than a running audit of what's essential, written by someone who did the experiment rather than just proposing it.

## Section 1 — Economy, taken literally

The book opens not with nature writing but bookkeeping: exact costs for lumber, nails, and the year's food, set against exactly how few hours of labor it took to earn them. Thoreau's real argument in "Economy" isn't asceticism for its own sake — it's that most people never run this calculation at all, and so spend a whole life "laying up treasures" and calling the inherited farm, the debt, the accumulation "necessity" when it was a choice nobody checked.

## Section 2 — Why he actually went

Stated as plainly as anything in the book: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life... and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived." The famous injunction to "simplify, simplify" isn't minimalism as aesthetic — it's Thoreau's proposed method for actually finding out what a life is for, by first cutting away everything that isn't required to ask the question.

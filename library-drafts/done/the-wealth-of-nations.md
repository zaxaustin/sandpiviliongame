## Summary

Adam Smith's 1776 founding text of modern economics — building the case, from ordinary observation rather than abstract theory, for why specialization and free exchange produce more wealth than a nation trying to plan or hoard its way there. A natural fit alongside this shelf's other founding arguments (Plato's justice, Aristotle's virtue, Marcus Aurelius's duty): a single, patiently-built claim about how people and societies actually work.

## Section 1 — The pin factory

Smith's most famous argument starts absurdly small: one untrained worker might make one pin a day; ten workers splitting the eighteen separate steps of pinmaking between them can make 48,000. Division of labor, not effort or genius, is what multiplies output — and Smith builds his entire theory of national wealth outward from this one concrete workshop example rather than starting with abstraction.

## Section 2 — Why this belongs on a shelf about running things

Beyond the famous "invisible hand" line most people know secondhand, the book is really a sustained argument about incentive, specialization, and trade-offs — directly useful to anyone thinking concretely about running an organization or a budget, not just a historical curiosity. Worth reading past the first few chapters; the pin factory is the on-ramp, not the whole argument.

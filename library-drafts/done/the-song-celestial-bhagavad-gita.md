## Summary

A prince, Arjuna, stands paralyzed on a battlefield facing kinsmen he must fight, and Krishna's answer — a discourse on duty, action, and the self — becomes the single most-read text in Hindu philosophy. Arnold's Victorian verse translation is the classic English door into it: a real philosophical argument delivered as poetry, not a dry treatise.

## Section 1 — Acting without grasping the outcome

The Gita's central teaching: act because the action is right and yours to do, without clinging to what the result will be — neither paralyzed by doubt nor driven by craving for the reward. Krishna's own image is the ocean, receiving every river without overflowing: the settled mind lets the world pour in without being moved by it.

## Section 2 — Why it opens this Library's Hindu shelf

Genuinely new ground here, not an echo of what's already shelved — its argument for equanimity through detached action runs parallel to the Stoic discipline in Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus (already on the Classics shelf) without being the same claim, and its picture of the Self (see the Upanishads, shelved alongside it) gives the metaphysics the Gita's ethics are actually built on.

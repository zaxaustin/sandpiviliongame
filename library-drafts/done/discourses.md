## Summary

A former slave turned Stoic teacher's actual classroom, recorded by his student Arrian: not a treatise but real lectures and arguments, opening from the single distinction Epictetus builds everything else on — what is "in our power" (judgment, desire, aversion) and what is not (body, property, reputation, other people). Pairs naturally with this Library's existing Marcus Aurelius, an emperor who studied Epictetus and wrote his own private notebook in direct response.

## Section 1 — The one distinction everything follows from

Book I opens by asking what faculty can judge itself, and answers: reason alone, the one thing the gods placed fully in our power. Everything Epictetus says afterward is downstream of this — pain, exile, even execution are only "intolerable" if we've wrongly filed them as ours to control. The famous line to Zeus he imagines: the gods gave a "small portion" of themselves — the faculty of using our own judgments — and told us to guard that as our only real possession.

## Section 2 — Philosophy tested against real deaths, not hypotheticals

What keeps this from reading as abstract theory is how often Epictetus reaches for actual Roman political prisoners under Nero and Vespasian — Agrippinus taking his exercise and cold bath on hearing he's condemned to banishment ("let us go to Aricia then, and dine"), Priscus Helvidius telling Vespasian plainly "it is your part to kill; it is mine to die, but not in fear." The philosophy is being demonstrated under the actual conditions it claims to prepare someone for, not argued for in the abstract.

# On the Second Floor

**Tradition:** Daoism
**License:** Original — Pavilion Commons
**Source URL:** https://suttacentral.net
**Attribution:** Pavilion stewards

## Summary

A short original poem on responsibility, written the same week visitors first started asking what's kept upstairs in the Library's "Restricted Section" — and the Monk started answering, kindly, never with a key.

## Section 1 — The poem

They say a door stands past the reading room,
sealed since before the first shelf was built,
guarding what plain shelves were thought too plain to hold.

Visitors ask the Monk what waits behind it.
He answers kindly, and never once with a key.

Not because the answer is too heavy to give —
but because the door was never load-bearing.
Take away the door: the weight doesn't move an inch.
It was always yours. It was always in your hands
the whole time you stood there, asking who'd let you in.

The uncarved block does not wait behind a lock.
Water does not request permission to find the sea.
What you were responsible for, you were responsible for
before you ever heard the rumor of a second floor.

Go on, ask the Monk again if you like.
He'll smile the same smile. The door stays shut.
That was always the whole teaching:
some doors are locked so you'll stop looking outward
for what was never anywhere but here.

## Section 2 — A steward's note

There is no second floor. There was never going to be one. The Monk will still, from time to time, decline to answer something by pointing you toward it — and he will never once tell you how to get in, because there is no in. That isn't a withheld feature; it's the joke, and also, entirely sincerely, the point: the Tao Te Ching already said the door you're looking for was never load-bearing. Nothing here was ever actually locked away from you.

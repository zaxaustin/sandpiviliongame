# Ten Acres Enough

**Tradition:** Practice
**License:** Public Domain (Project Gutenberg)
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/48753
**Attribution:** Edmund Morris

## Summary

An 1864 memoir-guide by a failed city businessman who moved his family onto ten acres of poor New Jersey soil, deliberately farmed small and intensively rather than trying to own more land than he could tend, and made it genuinely profitable through fruit-growing. A direct, real-numbers ancestor of the intensive small-scale, high-yield thinking modern permaculture and homesteading writing still argues for.

## Section 1 — Against the "more acres" instinct

The preface states the book's whole argument up front: the "great agricultural sin" of the era was buying more land than could actually be cultivated well, then working badly. Morris chose the opposite on purpose — ten acres, thoroughly worked — and wrote the book specifically for people who assumed, as most did then, that ten acres couldn't possibly be enough.

## Section 2 — Practical experience, not theory

True to its subtitle, this isn't agricultural philosophy — it's a specific account of what actually grew, what actually sold, and what actually supported a large family on a small plot, with real numbers rather than general encouragement. Worth reading as much for its underlying instinct (depth over acreage, real accounting over ambition) as for the specific 1860s fruit-farming detail.

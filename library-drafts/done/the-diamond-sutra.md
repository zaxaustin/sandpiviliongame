# The Diamond Sutra

**Tradition:** Mahayana
**License:** Public Domain (Project Gutenberg)
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64623
**Attribution:** Kumarajiva (Chinese translation, c. 401 CE) · tr. William Gemmell (1912)

## Summary

One of the great Prajnaparamita ("perfection of wisdom") texts of Mahayana Buddhism: a dialogue in which the Buddha answers his disciple Subhuti's question of how a bodhisattva should train the mind, arriving at the central, difficult teaching that even the ideas of "self," "being," and "dharma" must themselves be released — a wisdom sharp enough to cut through every remaining concept, "diamond" in name for exactly that reason.

## Section 1 — The frame

The sutra opens simply: the Buddha walks into Shravasti to beg for his morning meal, eats, washes, and sits down among his community before Subhuti stands and asks the question that drives the whole text — how does one who seeks enlightenment steady their own mind? Everything that follows is the Buddha's answer, given as a real conversation rather than a lecture.

## Section 2 — Wisdom that empties even itself

The sutra's central, repeated move is to grant a teaching and then immediately dissolve it: a bodhisattva should save all beings, yet see that "no being" is really saved; should give without attachment to any notion of giver, gift, or receiver; should perceive that even "the Buddha's teaching" is not a fixed thing to grasp. This isn't nihilism — it's precision about not mistaking the raft for the shore, a caution this Library's own Theravada shelf makes from a different angle but the Diamond Sutra pushes further than most texts are willing to.

## The Three Modes of Successful Intelligence

Real capability is not the same as performing well inside closed systems (tests, structured school environments). Drawing on Robert Sternberg's research on Successful Intelligence, every high-standard course must deliberately train three distinct modes that work together:

1. **Analytical** — Analyze, evaluate, compare, critique. Carefully represent and organize information. Record not only what happened, but what was expected, why it differed, and what the discrepancy implies (Faraday-style notebooks).

2. **Creative** — Generate ideas, reframe problems, question assumptions, redefine the question itself. Refuse to stay inside the first framing that feels comfortable.

3. **Practical** — Translate what you know into action that actually works under real conditions. Make the idea understandable and useful to different audiences. Implement, test, and persist until something functions in the world.

Sternberg's evidence showed that students taught to engage all three modes simultaneously significantly outperformed groups taught only one mode (or traditional memory methods) on real measures of achievement. Analytical ability alone produces people who do well when the rules are handed to them. All three together produce people who perform in open systems — careers, building, research, life.

**Requirement for every high-standard course:**
The design must make all three modes non-optional. Practices, artifacts, the daily commitment, the project, and the final gate must cycle the learner through analytical, creative, and practical work. Preferring only one mode is not enough for completion.

## The Seven Required Pieces

Every high-standard course in the Sand Pavilion must contain these seven elements. Nothing else is mandatory. These are.

> **Piece 2 was added 2026-08-14** at the steward's instruction, which is why
> this section is seven and not six:
>
> > *"we need to find people's baseline — where they are skill wise and what
> > they've done before. If they're complete beginners or if they have some
> > experience it's going to create different material for them. Let's make sure
> > we're taking pulse of someone's baseline before we build a course around
> > where they are, and we can use that as a reference for other people in the
> > future."*

### 1. Opening Intention
Before any modules exist, the creator (or the learner starting the course) must answer in writing:

- What do I actually want to learn?
- What is the real goal or question that makes this worth the work?
- What would concrete success look like?

This answer is kept visible for the entire course. It is the only legitimate reason the work continues. Ownership of the question is the foundation that makes the harder standards sustainable.

### 2. Baseline — where this person actually is

Before a single module is designed, take the pulse of what the learner already
has. A complete beginner and someone with real experience need **different
material for the same course**, and guessing which one you have is how a course
ends up too slow to bear or too fast to follow.

What the baseline records, in writing, before the modules exist:

- **What have you already done in this area?** Projects, jobs, courses, years —
  concrete things, not a self-rating.
- **What can you already do without looking it up?** The blank-page question,
  asked at the start instead of only at the end. This is what makes the final
  gate a measurement rather than a verdict.
- **Where did you last get stuck, and what stopped you?**
- **What do you have to hand?** Tools, parts, time in a day, a machine that can
  or cannot run a model.

**Two different things, and the standard needs both.** The course already
declares what *it* demands — `prerequisites`, `audience` and `level` in the
portable frontmatter, real fields since 2026-08-14. The baseline is what the
*person* brings. The design work is comparing them: what is missing becomes the
on-ramp, what is already owned is not re-taught, and a gap too wide to bridge is
said out loud rather than papered over.

**And the baseline is kept as a reference.** A completed baseline plus the course
it produced is the most useful thing one resident can leave another: it is
evidence of what this material actually takes *from a person starting there*.
Baselines travel only with deliberate transmission, like everything else — the
record is local, and nothing about a person's starting point is published by
default or shown to anyone as a ranking.

The baseline is taken again at the end. The difference between the two is the
honest measure of what the course did, and it belongs to the learner.

### 3. Theoretical Minimum
Identify the small set of load-bearing concepts and skills that must be mastered.
These are the items that will later be tested with pen and paper under blank-page conditions.
Everything in the course exists to serve this minimum.
Use the existing prompts (or local/cloud AI) to help surface them, then cut ruthlessly until only the essentials remain.

### 4. Daily Commitment + Reset Rule
The learner commits to one real focused action on committed days.

- Miss the commitment → progress through the course sequence returns to the beginning.
- All previous logs, notes, measurements, and written work are preserved.
- The learner may review the old material but may not copy-paste it back into the active course. They must re-do the work.

This is the repetition engine. It repeatedly cycles the learner through the three modes instead of letting them stay in the comfortable one. It is deliberately uncomfortable. It is how the standard is kept.

### 5. Structured Modules
Use the portable Markdown format already defined for the Pavilion:

- Clear modules with Objective, Body, Practice, Reflection, Artifact
- Practice that forces derivation, measurement, diagnosis, building, explanation, or reframing
- Free open tools and books named whenever they exist
- Artifacts that prove the work was actually done and that more than one mode was engaged

Design practices so that, across the course, analytical representation, creative reframing, and practical implementation all appear and are required.

### 6. Final Blank-Page Gate + Practical Proof
Before the course can be marked complete, the learner must:

- Demonstrate the Theoretical Minimum under blank-page conditions (no notes, no looking things up).
- Provide evidence that the knowledge works in the open (working artifact, measured result, or clear implementation).
- Show they can explain the core ideas to different audiences (at minimum: a precise version and a version useful to someone who needs it today).

Only after this combined gate is passed does the course count as finished for Independent Course Graduation or transmission.

### 7. Transmission
If the gate is passed, the work is polished so another person can walk it without the author present.
That polished package is what may be left as a bequest or Commons packet.
Transmission itself is a practical-intelligence act: the knowledge must survive contact with another mind.

## Front-End Experience in the Game (Recommended Shape)

The goal is clarity and accountability without turning the Pavilion into a complicated new app.

**Suggested surfaces:**

1. **Course Authoring Desk / Intention Desk**
   A calm place where a resident begins. First screen records the three intention questions. Until those answers exist, the rest of the course tools stay quiet.

2. **Baseline check**
   Straight after intention, before any modules are designed: what have you
   already done here, what can you do without looking it up, where did you last
   get stuck, what do you have to hand. Four questions, written by the person,
   kept beside the intention. Re-taken at the end so the difference is visible.
   Deterministic first — the questions and the record are code (rule 7); a model
   is only useful afterwards, for reading a baseline against a course's declared
   `prerequisites` and saying what to skip or shore up, and it never writes into
   the record without a press.

3. **Theoretical Minimum panel**
   After intention and baseline are set, a simple panel helps list the core items. This list becomes the permanent checklist for the final gate.

4. **Daily Check-in**
   Lightweight: "What one real thing did you do today?"
   Optional light tagging of primary mode (analytical / creative / practical) keeps the learner honest about balance.
   Missing a committed day triggers reset of sequence progress and surfaces the review requirement.

5. **Active Course Log**
   Modules are walked here. Full body text, practices, and artifacts are shown. Previous attempts remain visible in history so work is never lost, but the active sequence has restarted.

6. **Blank-Page + Practical Gate**
   Distinct final surface. Theoretical Minimum items under blank-page conditions, plus required evidence of a working artifact or real-world translation, plus short multi-audience explanation.

7. **Transmission / Export**
   Once complete, the familiar export / bequest / packet tools become available.

Minimum viable version remains small: intention recorded, baseline taken, daily check-in + commitment status, reset on broken commitment, preserved history, final gate against the Theoretical Minimum plus practical proof.

## Personal Accountability Mode (For the Steward and Anyone Who Wants It)

This system is being built first for the person who needs to be held to it.

In Personal Accountability Mode the rules are the same, only the grace is smaller:

- The resident explicitly activates the mode when starting the course.
- Weekends are included unless the resident declares otherwise at the start.
- The daily check-in is required.
- The reset on missed commitment is automatic.
- The final blank-page + practical gate is non-negotiable.
- The three modes are treated as required, not optional.

The logs still exist. The history is still there for review. The work must still be re-done.

This is the version the steward can turn on for himself so the Pavilion is not only a tool for others but a tool that holds its maker to the same standard.

## Institutional policy — the steward's ruling, 2026-08-14

The standard is **not optional for anything that claims to be a finished,
transmissible course.** It **is** optional for private personal tracking.

**For the individual**

- Starting a rich course, the first required act is recording the Intention
  questions. No Intention → the high-standard tools stay quiet.
- Daily check-in + commitment status is a first-class surface.
- Breaking the commitment resets sequence progress (logs preserved). Enforced by
  the software, not left to good intentions.
- The final gate (blank-page + practical proof + multi-audience explanation) is
  the only way a course can be marked Complete and become eligible for
  Independent Course Graduation or transmission.
- Personal Accountability Mode is an explicit, stricter contract the resident
  turns on.

**For the Pavilion as an institution**

- Only courses that have passed the full gate may carry the formal *Sand Pavilion
  High-Standard* or *Independent Course Graduation* recognition.
- Simple task-list courses remain available and useful, and are **clearly
  labelled as personal tracking**, never as completed high-standard work.
- The Library, Collections and transmission tools treat completed high-standard
  courses as first-class. Everything else is secondary.
- **The gate protects the name. We do not lower it to increase completions.**

**Sequence — the individual path first, community after.** Collections, Receive
Course, the daily check-in and reset, the blank-page gate, and the steward
actually walking a course under the rules. Only then a communal layer, and when
it comes it is an **opt-in publication and feedback layer on top of** the local
high-standard system, never a replacement for it. The Outer Pavilion stays
local-first.

**What holding the Pavilion to this actually means:** software enforcement of the
reset and the final gate; cultural enforcement in the language (nothing is called
complete until the gate is passed); and **personal enforcement by the steward
living under the same rules.** The last is the one that decides the other two —
the system will only be as serious as the person who built it is when no one is
watching.

## Closing Standard

We do not make courses hard out of cruelty.
We make them hard out of respect — for the material, for the people who will later trust a completed course, and for the person who is trying to become capable.

The resident who finishes under these rules has:

- Practiced the fundamentals repeatedly
- Returned after failure
- Demonstrated the core under blank-page conditions
- Produced something that works in the open
- Explained it so another person can use it

That combination — analytical rigor, creative reframing, and practical implementation — is what produces solid comprehension and real ability. It is also what can make a self-directed path feel more owned, more integrated, and in important ways more demanding than the average classroom experience.

That is the reputation the Sand Pavilion is building.

If you are creating a course, these pieces are the deal.
If you are walking a course, these pieces are the deal.
If you are the steward, these pieces are the deal you are making with yourself.
